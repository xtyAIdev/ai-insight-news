Time series analytics has been widely applied across various domains. 2. Point-in-Time Encoders for Earnings Calls

Text encoders turn earnings calls into vectors for search, for language models that answer from retrieved passages, and for forecasting models. A live retriever can use an encoder trained once on all available text, as finance retrievers are, but a backtest needs an encoder that read no text written after the date of each vector. To address this look-ahead bias, from 412,248 calls we train vintages under four pretraining recipes, with yearly cutoffs from 2010 to 2024, and each vintage reads only text dated on or before its cutoff. We then fine-tune each vintage on the question-answer pairs of the calls themselves. The vintages we train on calls alone, with 33.8M parameters, retrieve answers on held-out calls better than the general retrievers we tested in every test quarter. Their vectors also raise by about half the rank correlation between next-day returns and forecasts built from past returns and firm size. When each vintage continues the previous one, a later vintage retrieves better on earlier quarters than the vintage available at the time, and a point-in-time language model that answers from the passages the later vintage retrieves inherits the gain. The 2024 vintage of our families also forecasts earnings surprises better, although the interval of this gain includes zero on calls that no vintage read. It forecasts returns no better, so point-in-time vintages matter most for retrieval and little for forecasts of returns.

3. LUMIR: Decoupled Multimodal Representation Learning from Heterogeneous Data for Next-POI Recommendation

As multimodal tourism-related information collected from heterogeneous data sources has become increasingly available, learning effective representations has attracted attention for improving recommendation performance. Existing approaches often tightly couple multimodal fusion with the architecture and optimization objective of a specific downstream model, which limits the generality and reusability of the learned representations. To address these limitations, we propose LUMIR, a decoupled multimodal representation learning framework based on contrastive learning. Multimodal information collected from heterogeneous data sources is first semantically unified into a shared textual representation space using large language models (LLMs). An interaction-aware fusion mechanism is then designed to model cross-modal interactions, while sequential signals are incorporated as a structural constraint to guide representation learning. To evaluate the learned representations, experiments are conducted on a multimodal point-of-interest (POI) dataset under the next-POI recommendation setting across multiple sequential recommendation backbones. The results show that LUMIR consistently outperforms existing multimodal fusion baselines in both accuracy-oriented and beyond-accuracy metrics, demonstrating that decoupling representation learning from downstream optimization provides an effective paradigm for learning reusable multimodal representations. 4. ABRR: Transforming Temporal Sentence Grounding with Wasserstein-Adaptive Refinement and Precision Ranking

Temporal Sentence Grounding (TSG) aims to localize temporal segments in untrimmed videos that correspond to natural language queries, a critical task for video understanding with applications in video retrieval, summarization, and human-computer interaction. Despite significant progress driven by transformer-based models, existing TSG methods suffer from two key limitations: quantization errors due to temporal downsampling, which misalign segment boundaries, and suboptimal ranking of predictions based on semantic matching rather than localization precision. To address these challenges, we propose the Adaptive Boundary Refinement and Ranking (ABRR) framework, a novel, model-agnostic post-processing approach that enhances the performance of any TSG model without retraining. 5. Multimodal Judges Flag, Humans Decide: A Review-and-Edit Framework for AI-Generated Product Video Ads

Generative pipelines can now turn every product page of an e-commerce catalog into a short video ad, but deciding whether such an ad is usable is not one good-or-bad call. We describe how we operationalized the review and editing of these ads so that both can keep pace with generation: reviewers go through each ad scene by scene and answer six questions, on the product, scene quality, pronunciation, script, sound and captions; judges built from vision-language, speech and language models file each finding under the matching question, and reviewers accept or reject each finding and add what the judges missed. Each confirmed issue then opens an edit tool matched to it. The questions and the routing from a finding to its fix do not depend on the models that generate the ad. Across 120 ads from our pipeline, reviewed with judge findings on screen, reviewers marked the product wrong on 60% of scenes, and script, caption or sound problems on under 10% each. On 60 of these ads, they marked the product wrong on 58% of scenes, far more than the 26% marked by two reviewers who later saw the ads with the judges hidden; one reviewer who saw the ads both ways marked 57% with judge findings and 19% without.
