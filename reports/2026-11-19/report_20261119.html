2. Does the Action Matter? Probing Action-Conditioned Semantic Forecasting as a World Model for Autonomous Driving

World models are increasingly used in autonomous driving to predict how a scene evolves under the ego vehicle's actions, supporting counterfactual reasoning and model-based planning. However, existing evaluations focus on prediction fidelity and planning performance, and rarely verify that the predictions depend on the conditioning action. In this paper, we study action dependence in action-conditioned semantic forecasting, which predicts future semantic segmentation from past segmentations and the ego action. We compare a forecaster with feature-wise and input-level action conditioning against an identical model without action input, and measure action dependence with test-time action interventions scored by standard segmentation metrics. The evaluation is validated in a synthetic environment with known action-dependent dynamics and applied to Cityscapes and BDD100K, both within each dataset and across the two vehicle platforms. Experiments show that the executed action improves mid-term mIoU by at most 2.5 points on Cityscapes and 0.6 points on BDD100K, whereas a wrong action reduces it by up to 23.2 and 7.0 points, below the model without action input. 3. Jailbreak Detection on Large Language Models via Contrastive Prototype Structures

Jailbreak attempts and harmful prompts remain a challenge for the safe deployment of large language models. We study whether contrastively learned representations can support safety detection without training an additional downstream classifier. We propose Jailbreak Detection with Prototype Inference (JDPI), which uses binary safety labels to construct harmful and benign prototypes and makes predictions from relative prototype distances. JDPI uses only binary safety labels for encoder adaptation, prototype construction, and validation. In our setup, it uses fewer labeled examples than the classifier baseline (Method A) by avoiding the separate split used to train the downstream classifier. Across four benchmarks covering different input formats, contrastive fine-tuning improves JDPI's detection accuracy by 9.1-18.0 percentage points. Using the same encoder, JDPI achieves slightly higher ROC-AUC on one benchmark and stays within 0.017 of the baseline across all four. With dataset-specific adaptation, JDPI exceeds the released Llama Guard 3-8B baseline by 0.173-0.310 in ROC-AUC across the four benchmarks. In multi-turn replay on TomGibbs, JDPI uses accumulated user history as the conversation unfolds, achieving higher conversation-level accuracy than when it uses the current turn alone. Across 12 directed transfers, JDPI outperforms the classifier by an average of 1.59 percentage points in accuracy, demonstrating stronger cross-dataset robustness.

4. Who are you? The Personal Imitation Game

Large Language Models (LLMs) now perform strongly on Alan Turing's 1950 Imitation Game, so we propose a harder challenge, the Personal Imitation Game, which asks whether a machine can replicate a specific individual in conversation rather than a generic human. Testing with 30 participants, we establish a human baseline and report the identity signals interrogators rely on: stylometric features, personality traits, and biographical facts. To demonstrate the framework, we design an LLM-based system that plays the game for one of the paper's authors, drawing on an autoethnographic dataset spanning ten years of personal digital communication. In a 20-person deception study, interrogators choose our system over the author's own friends in 7 of 10 rounds, and the author over our system in 13 of 20; neither rate is distinguishable from chance at this sample size. We consider design improvements, applications of personal AI agents, and implications for identity and privacy.

5. Ground, Iterate, Trace: Decomposing AI Research Ideation

Large language models have been widely used to generate plausible research ideas, yet it remains unclear what makes automated ideation effective and how generated novel proposals arise from prior scientific work. We introduce the Creative AI Researcher (CAR), an agentic framework combining literature grounding, iterative development and explicit conceptual operators. CAR constructs a semantic knowledge graph from retrieved literature and equips an ideation agent with graph-retrieval tools and four operators: bisociation, analogy, exploration and elaboration. Each operator call is checked against structural acceptance conditions and recorded, producing a trace of intermediate transformations and declared literature sources. 大模型已被广泛用于生成看似可行的研究想法，但自动化构思为何有效、新颖提案如何从既有科学工作中演化而来，仍不清楚。为此，6 位作者提出 Creative AI Researcher（CAR）——一个结合文献 grounding、迭代开发与显式概念算子的 agentic 框架：它从检索到的文献构建语义知识图谱，并为构思 agent 配备图检索工具和 bisociation、analogy、exploration、elaboration 四个算子，每次算子调用都需通过结构性接受条件校验并被记录，从而留下中间变换与所声明文献来源的完整痕迹。在 40 个 LiveIdeaBench 研究主题和两个执行基准上、以两种语言模型骨干评测，CAR 的方法学新颖性（MN）显著高于单次提示与 The AI Scientist，与 Research Ideation Agent 的 MN 无显著差异，但可行性得分在两种骨干上均更高；在共享实验执行器下，CAR 在 FML-bench-Lite 与 BioXArena 上取得所评测 AI 方法中最高的成对胜率。十条件消融实验显示，两种骨干上 CAR 的 MN 点估计均优于一步式提案，但经多重比较校正后没有任何 MN 对比仍显著，因此结果尚不足以确立显式算子带来一致的质量收益。这意味着科研构思正在从一次性提示生成转向过程可分解、来源可追溯的工作流：把新颖性拆成可校验的概念操作，才有可能回答“想法为何有效、新在哪”。
