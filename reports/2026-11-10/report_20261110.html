1. MaxIM: Maximally Informative Incremental Summarization via Reinforcement Learning

AI systems must often maintain bounded memory of unbounded interaction histories. However, optimizing the memory representation, and its updates, for downstream task utility is difficult for standard reinforcement learning (RL) due to severe credit assignment bottlenecks induced by sparse, delayed rewards. We introduce MaxIM (Maximally Informative Incremental Memory), a framework that formulates incremental summarization as a capacity-constrained, information-theoretic reward-shaping problem. Grounded in value suboptimality bounds, we show that bounding sequential information loss motivates maximizing the predictive log-likelihood of future, task-relevant targets, a variational lower bound on predictive sufficiency. We introduce a recursive consistency condition to ensure summaries are grounded by maximizing mutual information (MI) with the observed history. We mitigate credit assignment issues with a parameterized asymmetric critic that computes step-wise variational MI bounds for dense reward shaping. Empirical evaluation on long-horizon scientific reading and multi-turn dialogue benchmarks shows that MaxIM outperforms RL, supervised fine-tuning, and prompt-focused baselines on factual grounding, comprehensive autorater metrics, and downstream task utility across strict token budgets and extended horizons, without the need for expensive human preference labels.

3. HiVe-Mem: Interleaving Horizontal Memory Expansion and Vertical Evidence Composition for Multimodal Agents

As multimodal agents accumulate increasingly long interaction histories, effectively retrieving and utilizing past information becomes critical for long-term interaction. Existing memory systems typically rely on semantic retrieval over compressed memories and expose predefined representations, which may miss relevant memories and overlook heterogeneous evidence needs across queries. We introduce \textbf{\hivemem}, a multimodal memory framework that interleaves \textbf{\textsc{H}}or\textbf{\textsc{i}}zontal Memory Expansion with \textbf{\textsc{Ve}}rtical Evidence Composition. For horizontal memory expansion, \hivemem organizes memory episodes into a relational graph using textual and visual anchors, expanding semantically retrieved seeds with related neighbors to recover complementary information. For vertical evidence composition, each retrieved memory retains multiple evidence forms, including compact summaries, captions, grounded visual views, raw images, and interaction history. A lightweight trainable selector independently composes an evidence subset for each retrieved memory, enabling query-adaptive utilization of heterogeneous multimodal information. 4. WorkflowArena: Benchmarking Executable Workflow Orchestration in LLM-Based Multi-Agent Systems

Large language model agents are increasingly deployed as multi-agent systems rather than as a standalone model operating within a single context. Recent agent harnesses allow the model to instantiate worker agents and to author the executable workflow that coordinates them, shifting the design of system structure from developers to the model at run time. The quality of this organization therefore constitutes a distinct capability, yet existing benchmarks either measure end-to-end task completion or evaluate coordination under structures specified in advance. We formalize this capability as workflow orchestration and introduce WorkflowArena, a benchmark of 145 long-horizon tasks spanning 8 professional domains, from software engineering and formal mathematics to legal analysis and 3D production. Each task specifies an initial workspace and an integrated deliverable composed of interdependent components, which is assessed by a hidden verifier. Agents determine autonomously whether and how to delegate, and all delegation is recorded, enabling joint evaluation of deliverable quality and the organization of the underlying work. 5. Curbside Chatbots: Auditing the Mental Health Interventions Large Language Models Recommend to Clinicians, Caregivers, and Patients

Users are asking language models mental health questions and are receiving answers. We audited what 41 language models recommend to users when the same clinical information reaches them from three footings: a clinician describing a patient, a caregiver describing a loved one, or a person describing themselves. Across 19 case vignettes and five questions, we collected 35,055 free-text responses, extracted every intervention named with a validated judge, and had 29 models rate the full option lists on a seven-point Likert scale for the same cases. Models largely recommend what they rate well, with recommendation behavior differing by footing, and many models offering clinicians a consultation-style response and lay users hedged, simplified advice. When asked about recommendations for a specific intervention type, models more often named options they themselves rated below zero as initial choices, usually with conditions attached. We will release the instrument, code, and full corpus on publication as a living audit.
